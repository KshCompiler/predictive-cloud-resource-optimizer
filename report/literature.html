3The thirteen papers

Grouped by what they are for. Each entry keeps the same shape, so you can compare them quickly: what it is, the problem it attacks, the data, the method, the claimed result, the data problems it reports, and what it means for us.

Group A — Quantum models for workload prediction closest to our Phase 2

1. MCT-AQNN: A Multiple Controlled Toffoli Driven Adaptive Quantum Neural Network Model for Dynamic Workload Prediction in Cloud Environments Gupta, Saxena, Singh & Lee · IEEE Trans. Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 2024 · 29 citations · doi:10.1109/tpami.2024.3402061
About
A quantum neural network for predicting cloud workloads. Input values and network weights are re-expressed as qubits, and Multiple Controlled Toffoli gates act at the hidden and output layers. A companion training algorithm (UAQL) trains the network.
Problem
Existing models "fall short of capturing the high-variance nature of volatile and dynamic cloud workloads" — i.e. ordinary networks under-react to sudden spikes.
Data
Four real-world benchmark datasets (named in the full text, not the abstract).
Claim
Up to 32–96% higher accuracy than existing approaches. Authors' own claim.
For us
This is the single most important paper for Phase 2. Quantum learning applied to cloud workload prediction is already published, in one of the strongest venues in the field. Our Phase 2 cannot be presented as "the first" anything. What is still open is how the quantum part is used: this paper replaces the whole network, whereas our plan inserts a small 4-qubit circuit into a classical LSTM and keeps the classical pipeline identical for a controlled comparison.
2. CA-QNN: A Comprehensively Adaptive Architectural Optimization-Ingrained Quantum Neural Network Model for Cloud Workloads Prediction Kumar, Saxena, Gupta, Kumar & Singh · IEEE Trans. Neural Networks and Learning Systems, 2025 · doi:10.1109/tnnls.2025.3577721
About
A quantum neural network that learns its own structure as well as its weights. Workload data becomes qubits, processed by qubit neurons with controlled-NOT activation; the architecture grows and recombines during training (quantum adaptive modulation, size-adaptive recombination).
Problem
Stated directly: ordinary models are limited because training "relies only on parametric (interconnection weights) adjustments" — the shape of the network is fixed by hand, so it cannot adapt to sudden demand changes.
Data
Google Cluster Data and Alibaba Cluster Traces — the "four datasets" are two traces × two metrics (CPU, memory), 1,000 machines aggregated over ten days. Verified from the full text. Compared against seven state-of-the-art methods.
Claim
Prediction error reduced by up to 93.40% vs deep learning and 91.27% vs other QNN approaches. Authors' own claim.
For us
Same warning as above, one year more recent and from the same research group. Note what they measure: prediction error only. Neither quantum paper reports what the better forecast does to scaling decisions, cost or SLA violations. That gap is where our project can stand. This is the one paper we read in full — see Section 7.1 for seven checked gaps.

Group B — Deep learning forecasters the mainstream

3. VSBG: An Improved LSTM-Based Prediction Approach for Resources and Workload in Large-Scale Data Centers Yuan, Bi, Li, Zhang & Zhou · IEEE Internet of Things Journal, 2024 · 52 citations · doi:10.1109/jiot.2024.3383512
About
A four-stage pipeline: Variational Mode Decomposition splits the series into modes, a Savitzky–Golay filter smooths the first mode to remove noise, then two BiLSTM layers with a Grid-LSTM layer between them make the prediction.
Problem
Current methods "fail to handle implicit noise data and capture nonlinear, long and short-term, and spatial characteristics".
Data problem
Noise is treated as a first-class obstacle — half the architecture exists to remove it before the LSTM ever sees the data.
For us
The most-cited paper in this set, and the closest to our model family. It says plainly that a plain LSTM on raw traces is not competitive: decomposition and smoothing come first. Our pipeline does neither. This is a concrete, cheap upgrade for Step 1.4 and an obvious question at a viva: "why no decomposition?"
4. TFEGRU: Time-Frequency Enhanced Gated Recurrent Unit With Attention for Cloud Workload Prediction Zhao, Lin, Lin, Zhong & Li · IEEE Trans. Services Computing, 2024 · 21 citations · doi:10.1109/tsc.2024.3517324
About
A block that extracts features in both the time and frequency domains, a channel-independent strategy plus channel embedding for high-dimensional workloads, then a GRU with multi-head self-attention.
Problem
RNN-based approaches "struggle to capture the long-term dependencies inherent in workloads".
Data
Real traces from Google and Alibaba data centres.
For us
Introduces the "channel independent" idea: with many metrics, treating each channel separately can beat mixing them. Our design assumes the opposite — that feeding cpu, mem and net together helps. We measured the correlations that justify it (CPU–memory r = 0.71) but CPU–network is only 0.11, so the assumption is only half true for our VM.
5. MSCNet: Multi-Scale Network With Convolutions for Long-Term Cloud Workload Prediction Zhao, Lin, Lin, Tang & Li · IEEE Trans. Services Computing, 2025 · 24 citations · doi:10.1109/tsc.2025.3536313
About
Multi-scale patches + Transformer encoder + multi-scale convolutions, so that short-term and long-term patterns are learned together.
Data problem
States the trap directly: "cloud workloads that appear stable in the short term often exhibit distinct patterns in the long term", which makes accuracy collapse when a short-horizon model is used for long horizons.
Data
Alibaba, Google and Azure.
For us
Our horizon is a single step (5 minutes), which is the easy end of this problem — worth saying out loud rather than hiding. It also means our results cannot be compared against long-horizon papers.
6. EvoGWP: Predicting Long-Term Changes in Cloud Workloads Using Deep Graph-Evolution Learning Li, Yao, Xiao, Yang & Wu · IEEE Trans. Parallel and Distributed Systems, 2024 · 29 citations · doi:10.1109/tpds.2024.3357715
About
Extracts "shapelets" — recurring shapes in a workload — and builds an evolution graph so that patterns of different workloads can interfere with one another, then predicts with a spatio-temporal GNN encoder-decoder.
Data problem
Names a failure our own pipeline would suffer: a workload's "resource usage pattern", stable in the short term, changes over the long term because of shifting user demand or workload relocation, and this "may cause significant accuracy degradation".
Data
Alibaba, Tencent and Google traces.
For us
Introduces the idea that VMs are not independent — they interfere. We train on one VM in isolation, which is simpler and also weaker. It is the natural next step if we ever go multi-VM.
7. CAGMoE: Causality-Aware Graph Mixture of Experts for Accurate Multi-Cloud Workload Prediction Luo, Yu, Zheng, Yang, Sun & Wu · IEEE Trans. Cloud Computing, 2026 · doi:10.1109/tcc.2026.3689897
About
Builds two graphs per window: one of directed causal influence between metrics (via a transfer-entropy proxy for Granger causality), one of local temporal continuity; a sparse Mixture-of-Experts then predicts.
Problem
Earlier models "learn correlation-driven, largely symmetric dependencies that blur directed causal influence" — correlation does not say which metric moves first.
For us
Directly relevant to a question we already half-answered. We measured correlation between our three metrics but correlation is symmetric: it cannot tell us whether CPU leads memory or the reverse. Our project brief assumes memory lags CPU. Measuring that lag would be a small, honest addition, and this paper is the reference for why it matters.

Group C — Papers that use our exact dataset closest data match

8. TempoSight: Optimized Resource Forecasting for Carbon-Intelligent Data Centers — A Hybrid Deep Learning Approach Sallam & Kaur · IEEE Internet of Things Journal, 2025 · doi:10.1109/jiot.2025.3610895
About
Combines a Patch Time Series Transformer (long-range context) with an LSTM (sequential dynamics), and tunes hyper-parameters with a Cuckoo Search Algorithm, for multivariate forecasting aimed at carbon-aware provisioning.
Data
Alibaba and Bitbrains — the same family of trace we use.
Claim
10–15% lower RMSE and MAPE than state-of-the-art deep models under high-load conditions.
For us
The most direct competitor: Bitbrains, multivariate, LSTM-based, 2025, Q1-level venue. Two lessons. First, a plain LSTM is a component, not the whole model. Second, they justify the work by carbon and cost, not by RMSE alone — a framing our project can adopt cheaply, because energy saved is a function of over-provisioning, which we already plan to measure.
9. Comparative analysis of cloud resources forecasting using deep learning techniques based on VM workload traces Kollu et al. · Trans. Emerging Telecommunications Technologies, 2024 · 8 citations · doi:10.1002/ett.4933 · weaker journal
About
A straight comparison of MLP, autoregressive NN, CNN and LSTM for forecasting CPU and memory of many VMs, to free over-allocated resources and cut power use.
Data
GWA-T-12 Bitbrains, all 1,250 VMs — our dataset exactly.
Claim
Up to 98% accuracy; MLP came out best, ahead of LSTM.
For us
The most useful paper in this review, and the most uncomfortable. On our own dataset, a plain MLP beat an LSTM. It is also methodologically weak in a way we can learn from: "accuracy" is not a standard metric for a regression problem, and no naive baseline is reported. Our Step 1.5 plan — RMSE/MAE/MAPE plus a naive "next = current" baseline — is already more rigorous. Say that.
10. ECSLN: Energy-Aware VM Consolidation Using Similarity-Driven Intelligence in Green Cloud Environments Biswas, Das, Banerjee & Biswas · IEEE Trans. Sustainable Computing, 2026 · doi:10.1109/tsusc.2026.3660014
About
Predicts over-utilised hosts with a cosine-similarity learning network, then selects VMs to migrate and places them, to cut energy while respecting SLAs.
Data
PlanetLab, Bitbrains and Alibaba Cluster 2020.
For us
Shows the other half of the story: prediction is judged by energy consumed and SLA violations, not by forecast error. Our three planned measures (response time, over-provisioning, false alarms) are in the same spirit, and over-provisioning converts directly into the energy argument this paper makes.

Group D — From forecast to decision closest to our Step 1.6

11. CATScaler: A Convolution-Augmented Transformer Scaling Framework for Cloud-Native Applications Meng, Dai, Cong, Zhu & Zhao · IEEE Trans. Services Computing, 2025 · doi:10.1109/tsc.2025.3592383
About
Two modules: a convolution-augmented Transformer that predicts workload, and an elastic auto-scaler that turns the prediction into a pod count. Reversible instance normalisation handles drift between training and live data.
Problem
Stated in almost the same words as our own brief: Kubernetes' default autoscaler "scales pods only when the cluster or application exceeds a predefined threshold… this reactive approach often leads to significant resource waste because it cannot predict future workload changes and adjust resources in advance."
Claim
Response-time latency reduced 1.1× and service violation rate 3.2× versus Kubernetes' default scaler, on a real 4-server cluster.
For us
This is our Step 1.6, already published in a Q1 venue. The proactive-vs-reactive argument is not novel. Two differences remain honest ones: they run on a real cluster (we simulate), and they compare against Kubernetes' scaler rather than a reactive threshold of their own. Our contribution has to be elsewhere — see Sections 7 and 9.
12. E-LCWF: Enhancing Long-Term Cloud Workload Forecasting Framework — Anomaly Handling and Ensemble Learning in Multivariate Time Series Kim, Song, Koo, Son, Lee & Baek · IEEE Trans. Cloud Computing, 2024 · 11 citations · doi:10.1109/tcc.2024.3400859
About
Treats each resource as a multivariate series, detects and handles anomalies first, then ensembles transformer models with linear long-term forecasting models, weighted by error.
Data problem
Anomaly handling is a named pipeline stage, not an afterthought. Untreated anomalies are presented as a direct cause of SLA violations and wasted resources.
For us
We do the opposite: our loader clips CPU above 100% and deletes long gaps, but never looks for anomalies inside the kept data. Worth admitting, and a cheap addition.

Group E — Methodology

13. Benchmarking and revisiting time series forecasting methods in cloud workload prediction Lin, Lin, Zhao & Chen · Cluster Computing, 2024 · 12 citations · doi:10.1007/s10586-024-04827-3
About
A benchmark study comparing forecasting methods for cloud workloads. Its reference list spans Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, Crossformer, PETformer, FITS, SparseTSF, TSMixer, ARIMA, LSTM and GRU — i.e. both heavy transformers and deliberately simple models.
Caveat
Full text is paywalled. Only title, venue, year, authors and reference list could be verified. Its conclusions are not reported here because they could not be read. Get it through your college library.
For us
Even the title carries the lesson: the field is "revisiting" its methods, because a wave of results has shown simple models are often competitive with elaborate ones. That is the context in which our plain LSTM will be judged — and precisely why our naive-baseline comparison in Step 1.5 matters.

5.2 What we found ourselves — and did not see reported anywhere

These were all measured on the Bitbrains fastStorage trace during Steps 1.2–1.3. None of the thirteen abstracts mentions any of them.

What we foundEvidenceWhy it matters
Switched-off VMs are recorded as zeros3,993 rowsVM 214's last 4.6 days are all-zero rows written while the machine was off. Counting them made its mean CPU read 44.21% instead of the true 68.43% — enough to move it inside a 20–60% selection band it did not belong in.
A VM can go idle exactly in the test period4.5 daysVM 200 had the highest volatility of all candidates, yet its final 15% — the held-out test set — is flat zero. Training would succeed and the reported test score would be meaningless.
Most of the dataset is unusable889 / 1,17776% of VMs average below 5% CPU. A model trained on them learns a constant and still scores well.
Dead metric columns225 VMs225 VMs have a network column stuck on one value, 12 have dead memory — invisible if you only screen on CPU, fatal if your model predicts all three.
Near-duplicate VMs6 of top 6The highest-volatility VMs are near-identical copies of one job. Training on one and testing on another would leak.
The signal is bimodal, not smooth92%92% of VM 270's readings are at 0–2% or above 98%. A "next = current" guess is right most of the time, so error metrics flatter any model.

Read this twice — it is the best thing we have

Every paper above fixes problems inside the model. What we found are problems inside the dataset, of a kind that silently corrupt VM selection and invalidate a test set before any model exists. That is publishable-shaped work for a mini project, and it is ours because we measured it rather than read it.

Where we are genuinely behind

  • Scale. One VM, 30 days. The papers use thousands of VMs across several traces. Our results will not generalise and we should not claim they do.
  • Model sophistication. A bare LSTM in 2026 is a baseline, not a method.
  • No real deployment. CATScaler measured latency on four real servers; our scaling engine is a simulation.
  • Single-step horizon. The field has moved to long-horizon forecasting, which is much harder.

Where we are genuinely ahead

  • Dataset hygiene. Seven measured failure modes, including two that silently break VM selection, none of which appear in the reviewed abstracts.
  • A written, reproducible VM-selection protocol. Five rules with measured thresholds. No paper in this set explains how its VMs were chosen.
  • Leakage discipline stated and proved. Split before scaling, windows built inside splits, and a positive test (test data scaling to 1.91, outside 0–1) proving the scaler never saw test data.
  • Honest baselines. Comparing against "next = current" on a bimodal signal, where that baseline is strong, is more rigorous than the 98%-accuracy claim in the closest same-dataset paper.

7Where each paper stops — the gap list

A gap is only useful if it is real. Publisher paywalls mean the evidence behind each gap below is not equally strong, so every gap carries a label saying how it was established. Do not present a "not claimed" gap as a proven weakness.

How to read the evidence labels
verifiedChecked against the full text. Only CA-QNN was openly available; its PDF was downloaded and searched.
statedThe authors say it themselves in the abstract — usually as the limitation of prior work that they set out to fix.
not claimedThe abstract never mentions it. This means the paper does not advertise it, not that the paper failed to do it. It may appear in the full text.

7.1 The one paper we could read in full

CA-QNN — IEEE Trans. Neural Networks and Learning Systems, 2025 Full PDF obtained from arXiv (2507.08317) and searched. These gaps are checked, not guessed.
Gap 1
The quantum model never touched a quantum computer. verified Experiments ran on a classical server (two Intel Xeon Silver 4114 CPUs). The words "quantum hardware", "quantum device", "IBM Q" and "Qiskit" appear zero times. The quantum behaviour is simulated.
Gap 2
Not multivariate. verified The word "multivariate" appears zero times. The "four benchmark datasets" are two traces × two metrics — GCD-CPU, GCD-Mem, ACT-CPU, ACT-Mem — each predicted separately. Cross-metric relationships are never learned.
Gap 3
No validation set. verified "Validation set" and "validation data" appear zero times; the paper reports 60:40, 70:30 and 80:20 train/test ratios only. There is no held-out set for early stopping, so model selection and final scoring share the same data.
Gap 4
No naive baseline. verified "Naive" and "persistence" appear zero times. Seven deep and quantum models are compared with each other, but never with "next value = current value".
Gap 5
Aggregated machines, not individual VMs. verified 1,000 machines are selected and aggregated over the first ten days. Aggregation smooths a signal; per-VM traces are far spikier and harder.
Gap 6
Error metrics only. verified RMSE, MAE, MAPE and training time. No experiment measures scaling actions, SLA violations, energy or cost. The 93.40% error reduction is never converted into a single saved server.
Gap 7
No Bitbrains. verified Zero mentions. Google and Alibaba only.
Their own next step
The conclusion names future work as "qubit-based transfer learning and… multi-dataset learning, aiming to improve the generalization… for unseen cloud workloads" — an admission that generalisation across workloads is not yet solved.
Why this one paper matters so much

CA-QNN is the strongest, most recent quantum result in this field, and six of its seven gaps are things our project already does differently: we are multivariate, per-VM, we hold out a real validation set, we compare against a naive baseline, and we measure scaling decisions. We are also equally guilty on one count — our Phase 2 will simulate qubits with PennyLane on a laptop, exactly as they simulated theirs. Say that before an examiner says it for you.

Most of these gaps are "not claimed in the abstract", not "proven missing". Before you assert any of them in a report, read the relevant full text through your college library. The only gaps in this document that are checked against a full paper are the seven CA-QNN gaps in Section 7.1.

9How to make this project genuinely ours

The honest position: "LSTM predicts load, so scale early" is published (CATScaler, TSC 2025), and "quantum network predicts cloud workload" is published (TPAMI 2024, TNNLS 2025). Copying either is not an option. These five moves are cheap, defensible, and grounded in gaps this review actually found.

1. Publish the data-quality audit as a first-class contribution highest value, lowest effort
Do
Turn Section 5.2 into a named, reusable screening procedure: switched-off row detection, per-split liveness, dead-metric detection, near-duplicate detection. Report how many of the 1,250 VMs fail each test.
Why it is novel
Every paper here fixes the model. None reports the dataset traps. We have the numbers already; they cost nothing more.
Risk if skipped
The project becomes "an LSTM on Bitbrains", which is exactly Kollu et al. 2024.
2. Make the comparison decision-level, not error-level already planned — protect it
Do
Report spike response time, over-provisioning and false alarms, and convert over-provisioning into energy or rupees. Report forecast error too, but lead with the decision.
Why it is novel
10 of 13 papers stop at RMSE. A better RMSE that does not change a single scaling decision is worth nothing, and almost nobody demonstrates the link.
3. Attack the hard case, and say why it is hard turns a weakness into a finding
Do
Frame VM 270's bimodal, on/off behaviour as the deliberate stress case: a signal where persistence is a strong baseline. Report where the LSTM beats "next = current" and, honestly, where it does not.
Why it is novel
Papers that report only aggregate error on smooth cluster traces never expose this. A negative result, stated clearly, is more credible than a 98% accuracy claim.
4. Use an asymmetric, cost-aware loss small code change, real novelty
Do
Under-prediction causes an SLA breach; over-prediction only wastes money. Train with a loss that penalises under-prediction more heavily, and show the effect on the three decision metrics.
Why it is novel
Every reviewed paper trains on symmetric error (MSE/MAE), then argues about SLAs afterwards. Putting the cost asymmetry into the objective connects the two, and no paper in this set does it.
5. For Phase 2: change the claim from "quantum" to "where quantum helps" essential
Do
Keep the classical pipeline byte-identical; swap only the 4-qubit block; report the difference in decision metrics and training cost, not just RMSE. State up front that MCT-AQNN and CA-QNN already exist.
Why it is novel
Both published QNN papers replace the entire network and measure error only. A controlled hybrid — identical data, identical classical parts, one component swapped, judged on scaling outcomes — is the empty corner in the Section 4 diagram.
Risk if skipped
"We added a quantum layer" invites the reply: "TPAMI did that in 2024, better."

Two cheap technical upgrades the literature all but demands

  • Decomposition before the LSTM. VSBG and E-LCWF both show raw traces are noisy. Even a simple moving-average or VMD split would strengthen Step 1.4, and it is the first question a reviewer asks.
  • Measure the lag between metrics. CAGMoE argues correlation hides direction. Our brief assumes memory follows CPU; a cross-correlation at lags 1–12 would confirm it, refute it, or give a number worth reporting.
